Small Caps, Super & Subscript

Real Unicode characters•and a straight answer about the missing ones

Why are some letters left alone?

Because the character does not exist. These three styles were never designed as complete alphabets — they were added to Unicode piecemeal for phoneticians, mathematicians and chemists, so the coverage follows what those fields needed. There is no small-capital Q or X anywhere in Unicode, no superscript lowercase q, and subscript is the thinnest set of all: no capitals at all and no b, c, d, f, g, q, w, y or z. Each card above lists its own gaps.

So what happens to a letter with no character?

It passes through exactly as you typed it. That is a deliberate choice: the alternative is an empty box, a question mark, or nothing at all, and a plain letter is the least broken of the four. It does mean a word containing q or x will look slightly inconsistent in small caps — typing that letter as a capital is usually the best-looking fix.

Is this the same as the small caps in a word processor?

No, and the difference matters. A word processor synthesises small caps by shrinking real capitals, which looks better but only exists inside that document. These are separate Unicode characters, so they survive being pasted into a bio, a username or a chat where no formatting is possible at all. The trade-off is coverage — see above — and that screen readers may announce them oddly, covered in fancy text and screen readers.
